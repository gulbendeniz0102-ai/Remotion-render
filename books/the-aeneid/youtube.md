## Description

```
The greatest poem any government ever paid for ends with its hero butchering a man who is begging on his knees — and then it just stops. No triumph, no founding, no Rome.

Rome commissioned a founding myth from Virgil and got a casualty report. In this deep dive we read The Aeneid not as a hero's journey but as literature's first sustained portrait of moral injury: pietas is not a virtue in this poem, it is the coping mechanism holding a traumatized man together — and the epic ends at the exact instant it fails.

We trace what the mission costs, book by book: Creusa lost in the smoke because he would not look back, Dido destroyed by an order he never chose ("I do not chase Italy of my own free will"), the prophecy of Rome that Aeneas exits through the gate of FALSE dreams, a shield engraved with a history he is not permitted to understand, the boys the founding consumes — and Turnus, wounded and begging, killed the moment Aeneas sees Pallas's sword belt.

Then the honest counterpoint: the bleak reading may be our projection, Virgil took Augustus's patronage, and he died before he could revise the ending. And the fact that reframes all of it — on his deathbed Virgil ordered the manuscript burned, and the emperor overruled him.
```
